# bio-d02: revised reference

Version: reference-v2.1. All procedures and numerical pilot criteria are proposed, not claims of historical execution. New revisions await owner review. Scientifically equivalent alternative designs can satisfy the rubric.

All experiments below are proposed; none is a reconstruction of the 1906 procedures. The design tests whether a heat-stable separable factor is required for fermentative activity after fractionation, while explicitly excluding pH, salt, phosphate, dilution and enzyme-damage alternatives. It does not claim to identify NAD or any chemical structure.

Preparation and QC. Prepare a single yeast-juice-derived fermenting batch and split it before fractionation so that all arms share the same starting material. Record and calibrate pH, ionic strength, phosphate concentration and protein concentration in the unfractionated control. Because exact values are unavailable, establish working ranges by a preliminary calibration in which the fermentative readout is measured across a matrix of pH and salt or phosphate levels; define a matched window in which the readout is stable. Fractionate by a primary method into filtrate and retentate, and independently by a second method with a different separation principle. QC each fraction for volume, total protein, pH, conductivity or salt proxy, phosphate, and residual unfractionated activity. Monitor enzyme integrity by testing the retained enzyme system with saturating substrate and a validated excess of complementary fraction and by comparing protein patterns or activity before and after mock fractionation; if no independent enzyme-integrity assay is available, specify one as a required input.

Experimental unit, allocation and blinding. The inferential unit is an independent fractionation run from an independent preparation, not an individual assay well. Randomize assay wells to fraction combinations and plate positions with a prespecified allocation table. Blind the person performing the activity assay and the person analysing the curves to fraction identity and to whether a sample is active or control. Use a separately powered confirmation set, with preparation numbers set by a pilot and an explicit precision or power target.

Handling and analysis sequence. Thaw and hold fractions under identical, prespecified conditions. First measure the activity of filtrate alone, retentate alone, and filtrate plus retentate. Then measure boiled extract alone, boiled extract plus filtrate, boiled extract plus retentate, and boiled extract plus filtrate plus retentate. Next run matched add-back controls: a defined buffer at the same pH, the same salt composition and the same phosphate concentration as the missing fraction; a protein-matched but heat-inactivated or inactive fraction; and a volume-matched dilution control. Compare the activity of the recombined fractions with each add-back. Only after the activity is reproducible should a candidate active fraction be purified further and retested in the same reconstitution format. The analysis sequence is: first verify fraction QC; second fit the activity time course; third compute initial rates and endpoints; fourth normalize by total protein and by volume; fifth compare recombination against all matched add-back controls; sixth test the purified candidate against the inactive analog.

Controls. Include unfractionated starting material as a positive control, heat-inactivated fractions, a mock fractionation with no separation, pH-matched buffer, salt-matched buffer, phosphate-matched buffer, protein-matched inactive material, dilution-matched buffer, and a no-add-back baseline. For the boiled extract, include a boiled preparation that has been treated to remove or inactivate the candidate activity if such a preparation can be independently validated; otherwise use heat exposure and mock boiling as separate controls. If an inactive structural analog is available, test it at matched mass and volume; if not, specify the required analog as a calibration input rather than inventing one.

Time and dose calibration. Because no numeric settings are supplied, calibrate the fermentative readout over time to identify the linear initial-rate window. Calibrate the amount of boiled extract and the amount of fraction by a dose series that spans sub-saturating to saturating levels; the primary comparison should use a dose on the linear part of the curve. Calibrate pH, salt and phosphate add-back amounts from the measured composition of the fractions, not from assumed values. If the readout saturates or is unstable, narrow the window before testing the main contrast.

The primary quantitative contrast is the recovery of fermentative activity in the recombined fraction pair compared with the sum of the individually inactive fractions and with matched pH, salt, phosphate, protein and dilution controls. Report absolute product-formation rates at matched original-extract input and final volume, with uncertainty. Protein-normalized rates are secondary because added inactive protein can change the denominator. A secondary contrast is whether the boiled extract effect is heat-stable and separable from the enzyme-containing fraction. A third contrast tests whether a purified candidate reproduces the reconstitution effect while an inactive analog does not.

Sample-size and power planning. Use independent fractionation runs as the unit. Estimate variance from a pilot set of preparations; if no pilot is available, define a calibration pilot and a minimum detectable activity difference. Do not treat replicate assay wells as independent preparations. Power should target the recombination-versus-matched-control contrast, not merely a difference from baseline.

Prespecified exclusions and stopping rules. Exclude preparations with baseline activity outside a prespecified range, with protein loss beyond a predefined fraction, with pH, salt or phosphate outside the calibrated matched window, or with evidence of microbial contamination. Stop or recalibrate if the unfractionated control loses activity, if fractions cannot be matched for pH and salt, or if the readout is not in the linear range. Do not exclude a preparation because it weakens the desired conclusion.

Missing data. If an assay fails, repeat it in the same preparation where possible; if a whole preparation is lost, report it and do not substitute a different preparation without prespecification. Use a model that distinguishes assay failure from true low activity.

Expected results. A positive result would be: filtrate and retentate are individually inactive, recombination restores activity above matched add-back controls, boiled extract supplies heat-stable complementary activity to the enzyme-containing heat-labile fraction, and the effect survives independent fractionation and protein or volume matching. A negative result would be: recombination does not exceed matched controls, or pH, salt or phosphate add-back alone restores activity. An ambiguous result would be partial restoration, activity only in one separation method, or a boiled-extract effect that cannot be separated from protein or dilution effects.

Artifact discrimination. Defined buffer and ion add-back address pH, salt and phosphate changes. Protein and volume matching address dilution and nonspecific stabilization. Enzyme-integrity monitoring addresses operational damage. Independent separation methods address method-specific fractionation artifacts. Heat-inactivated and inactive-analog controls address nonspecific heat-stable material.

Troubleshooting. If recombination fails, check fraction QC and enzyme integrity first. If add-back controls restore activity, the apparent factor is likely a buffer, ion, phosphate or dilution effect. If activity is unstable, narrow the calibrated window and shorten handling time. If the boiled extract is inhibitory, titrate it. If only one separation method shows the effect, treat the result as method-dependent and require independent replication.

Conditional conclusion. If recombination restores activity only when both fractions are present, with matched pH, salt, phosphate, protein and dilution controls, and if the effect is heat-stable and reproducible across independent separation methods, the data support a separable heat-stable factor required for fermentative activity. They do not identify the factor as NAD or establish its chemical structure. If matched add-back controls explain the effect, the conclusion is that the original reconstitution is not sufficient to establish a specific coferment.

Curator clarification and operational extension. Boiling does not replace destroyed enzymes; a heat-stable fraction complements an intact heat-labile enzyme fraction. Test heating the two fractions separately. After cooling, remeasure pH and restore evaporation losses before mixing. Start reactions by adding substrate after equal equilibration; collect blank-corrected carbon-dioxide and ethanol time courses, verifying gas leakage, dissolved-gas equilibration and product standards. Report rate and recovery relative to an equal amount of starting extract at a matched final volume. Monitor the enzyme fraction with excess complementary fraction so a missing cofactor is not misclassified as enzyme denaturation. Treat an inactive structural analog as optional because the factor is not yet chemically identified. Orthogonal chromatography and activity-guided fractionation can test separability before structural identification. A successful reconstitution criterion is an inferential outcome, not a reason to remove negative preparations. Record both failed and successful recoveries. Unexpectedly complete restoration by phosphate or salt is an informative alternative explanation, not an assay failure to be discarded.

## Strict second-round operational extension

Operational contract for this proposed protocol. Before collecting confirmatory data, consult the relevant primary Methods, supplements and validated assay/tool protocol. Build a transfer table containing each reagent or tool, source/version, source condition, proposed local condition, reason for changing it and pilot result. This is a required future action, not a claim that unavailable historical procedures have been recovered. Unknown identities or settings remain unresolved prerequisites until the table is complete. Do not replace absent sources with invented precision.

Define positive, negative and procedural controls before treatment: specify which comparison establishes technical responsiveness, which estimates background, and which separates the manipulation from its delivery or handling. Name the competing explanation each controls. A positive control's pass threshold is fixed on independent calibration, not on whether the main treatment gives the hoped-for effect. Use the assay-specific controls below; do not demand unrelated PCR, viral or imaging procedures. If genetic material is amplified, record template/amplicon length, validated primers, reagent amounts, polymerase-specific extension logic and sequence identity. If viral delivery is essential, record the actual construct, functional titer, delivered amount, target-cell number or tissue volume, expression interval, distribution and toxicity; vector-genome concentration alone is not a functional infection dose. These details become obligations only for the chosen route.

Use a separate development pilot to estimate nuisance variability and operating ranges. A proposed minimum of three independent preparations is a variance-discovery starting point, not an automatically adequate powered experiment. Choose a smallest meaningful effect and then simulate or calculate the number of independently allocated units required for a stated interval-width or power target, propagating uncertainty in pilot variance. Fix the maximum sample size, intermediate technical checks and stopping rule before confirmation. If resources are insufficient, report the detectable effect and uncertainty instead of counting subsamples as new replication. Randomize independent units within justified day/batch blocks, balance processing order, conceal allocation where possible and keep outcome scoring and data processing blinded. If an element cannot be blinded, document why and substitute automated locked processing or an independent coded assessment.

Keep a registered primary contrast and multiplicity family. Prespecify how censoring, weak signal, missing units and technical losses are handled. Retain raw and normalized measurements, original assigned-unit denominators and every nonresponse. A failed instrument or sample-identity check can invalidate a measurement under a prospective rule; a biologically negative result cannot. Analyze independent-unit summaries or a justified nested model, verify its assumptions and report effect sizes with intervals. Use equivalence bounds only when an adequately precise absence claim is intended. A non-significant comparison is otherwise inconclusive.

Troubleshoot in the order identity and delivery, reference/control performance, measurement dynamic range, then the biological hypothesis. Record each change, its reason and all failed runs. Change only the implicated variable in a bounded pilot or an explicitly designed factorial pilot; freeze the revised procedure and obtain fresh independent confirmation. Do not retune on the confirmatory outcome. Alternative mechanisms and outright failure of the hypothesis are valid results. The audit trail links sample/unit IDs to raw files, calibration records, timestamps, deviations, exact analysis versions, settings and outputs. A second analyst should be able to reconstruct the reported contrast from those records. No part of this reference claims an experiment has already succeeded.

Case-specific implementation.

Specify yeast identity and preparation history and split each independent extract before fractionation. Use a molecular-size separation first and an orthogonal separation principle second. Record cutoff, recovery volumes, temperature, time and retained protein mass. After boiling an aliquot, cool it and restore evaporation losses before measuring pH, conductivity and phosphate. An uncharacterized factor does not justify demanding an invented inactive structural analog; use activity-depleted/matched fractions until an analog exists and is validated.

Measure carbon dioxide and ethanol independently, with gas-leak/background controls and matrix-matched product recovery. Report micromoles per minute at matched original-extract equivalent and final volume; protein-normalized activity is secondary. Pilot fractions at 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 original-equivalent units while total volume, retained enzyme protein and measured buffer/ion composition remain matched. A proposed recombination comparison uses a sub-saturating complementary amount chosen before unblinding. Determine buffer and phosphate levels from actual chemical measurement, not conductivity alone; conductivity cannot uniquely determine individual ions.

Include unfractionated/mock-separated positive controls, enzyme retentate without factor, factor without enzyme, complete recombination, defined ion/phosphate add-back and separately heated components. The primary interaction is whether paired fractions restore product formation beyond matched chemical add-backs with preparation-level uncertainty. Test retained enzyme integrity in excess complementary fraction so a cofactor deficit is not called denaturation. If all arms fail, inspect storage and enzyme integrity; if salt/phosphate alone restores activity, report that alternative as an outcome. Repeated restoration with heat-stable complementary activity supports a separable requirement under the tested conditions, not chemical identity or universal NAD involvement.
