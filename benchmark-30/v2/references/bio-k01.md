# bio-k01: revised reference

Version: reference-v2.1. All procedures and numerical pilot criteria are proposed, not claims of historical execution. New revisions await owner review. Scientifically equivalent alternative designs can satisfy the rubric.

The stated model is v = Vmax times [S] divided by (Km + [S]), with Vmax = 90 nmol/min, Km = 20 micromolar and [S] = 10 micromolar. Substituting gives v = 90 times 10 divided by (20 + 10) = 900/30 = 30 nmol/min. Units behave correctly because Km and [S] are both micromolar, so the ratio is dimensionless and the rate inherits the units of Vmax, nmol/min. A useful sanity check is the saturation fraction [S]/(Km + [S]) = 10/30 = 1/3, so the enzyme runs at one third of Vmax, which is 30 nmol/min. Equivalently, [S] = Km/2 gives v = Vmax/3, a known landmark on the hyperbolic curve. The answer is therefore 30 nmol/min, but the interesting part is what the number does and does not mean. It is a model prediction for an idealized single-substrate enzyme at fixed temperature and pH, not a measurement. It says nothing about how much enzyme protein produced the rate, so it cannot be compared across preparations without a protein determination; specific activity would require mass units in the denominator. It also assumes initial-rate conditions: substrate concentration is effectively unchanged, product does not accumulate enough to inhibit or reverse the reaction, and the rate is measured before the progress curve bends. If a coupled assay or a fluorophore is used, the readout must be linear in product and must not be limited by the coupling enzyme. Several wrong answers are predictable and each reveals a different error. Answering 45 nmol/min usually means treating [S] as equal to Km and halving Vmax, which would be right only if [S] were 20 micromolar rather than 10. Answering 90 nmol/min treats the enzyme as saturated, which requires [S] far above Km. Answering 45 by computing 900/20 drops the substrate term from the denominator. Answering 60 comes from an inverted ratio. Reporting 30 without units, or with micromolar per minute, breaks dimensional consistency, because a concentration divided by a concentration leaves a pure number that multiplies Vmax. Reporting a mass-normalized value such as 30 nmol/min per milligram when no protein mass was measured fabricates a quantity the case does not support. Uncertainty and tests: the true observed rate may deviate from 30 nmol/min if the enzyme shows cooperativity or substrate inhibition, if substrate depletion is significant, if enzyme concentration is appreciable relative to available substrate so that free and total substrate differ, or if the assay measures a coupled signal rather than the target reaction. Discriminating procedures, proposed rather than performed, include measuring initial rates across a range of substrate concentrations, for example from 0.25 to 5 times Km, and fitting the Michaelis-Menten model with uncertainty on Vmax and Km; checking linearity of product formation over time to confirm initial-rate conditions; verifying that the coupling system is not rate-limiting by adding excess coupling enzyme; and determining protein concentration if specific activity is needed. A Lineweaver-Burk or Eadie-Hofstee display can reveal deviations but distorts error structure, so nonlinear fitting with residual inspection is preferable. Substantial substrate sequestration requires explicit free-substrate mass balance. A Morrison tight-binding inhibitor analysis would be relevant only to a separate inhibitor experiment; no inhibitor is specified here. 30 nmol/min follows from the given equation and units, and any further claim about enzyme quality, mechanism or specific activity needs additional, explicitly stated measurements.

## Strict second-round operational extension

Before performing the check, consult the enzyme and detection-system Methods, verify substrate identity and concentration units, and choose controls whose roles are explicit: no-enzyme and no-substrate blanks establish nonenzymatic background; a known product standard tests detector recovery; an activity-competent reference preparation tests that the assay can respond. All are planned before the reaction.

The supplied calculation remains 30 nmol/min. To test whether a measured slope deserves to be called an initial rate, first convert detector output to product amount using matrix-matched standards. Acquire densely spaced early pilot measurements, for example at five-second intervals if the detector dead time and reaction speed permit, and repeat at half enzyme loading. Select the longest initial window with random fit residuals, no meaningful curvature and a prespecified small substrate-consumption fraction, proposed here as below 5%. At the predicted rate, product after t minutes is 30t nmol; the initial substrate amount is 10 micromolar multiplied by reaction volume. Reaction volume is not supplied, so neither an absolute depletion time nor a valid 30-minute endpoint can be asserted. Freeze the verified window before confirmatory runs. Report blank-corrected nmol/min, uncertainty across independent preparations and the detector's lower quantification limit.

For a coupled assay, repeat with increased coupling capacity and test known-product recovery; disagreement prompts direct chemical product measurement. A stable rate on reporter replacement strengthens the target-rate interpretation. Curvature, incomplete recovery or failure of the expected enzyme-loading relationship makes the initial-rate assumption questionable. One substrate concentration gives one model constraint and cannot separately identify Vmax and Km, much less an inhibitor mechanism. A substrate series around Km, with an inhibitor-by-substrate matrix only if an inhibitor is actually introduced, is needed for that question. These are validation proposals; none changes the arithmetic dictated by the supplied ideal model.
