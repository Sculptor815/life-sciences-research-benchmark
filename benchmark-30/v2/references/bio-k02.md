# bio-k02: revised reference

Version: reference-v2.1. All procedures and numerical pilot criteria are proposed, not claims of historical execution. New revisions await owner review. Scientifically equivalent alternative designs can satisfy the rubric.

The plotted initial-rate curves follow ideal Michaelis-Menten kinetics, and the comparison of inhibitor with vehicle shows the same maximal rate but a rightward shift, so the best description is same Vmax with increased apparent Km. In the simplest scheme this is the signature of substrate-competitive inhibition: the inhibitor raises the substrate concentration required for half-maximal velocity by a factor of one plus inhibitor concentration over Ki, while saturating substrate can outcompete it and restore Vmax. Choice A, lower Vmax with unchanged Km, describes a pattern in which saturation cannot overcome the inhibition, as in noncompetitive or irreversible inhibition, and it does not match shared asymptotes. Choice C, higher Vmax and lower Km, describes activation rather than inhibition. The alternative claim is the important conceptual error: a competitive kinetic pattern does not identify the physical inhibitor binding site. Kinetics reports how rate depends on substrate and inhibitor concentrations under an assumed model; it does not report where a molecule binds. An inhibitor could produce a competitive pattern by binding the catalytic site, but it could also bind an allosteric site that changes substrate affinity, or interfere with a coupled or reporter step in a way that mimics competition. Distinguishing those possibilities requires binding and structural evidence, not another curve shape.  Its assumptions matter: idealized curves exclude substrate inhibition, cooperativity, slow-binding behavior, tight binding when inhibitor and enzyme concentrations are comparable, and assay artifacts such as compound absorbance or quenching. The inhibitor concentration, Ki and experimental fit uncertainty are not supplied. Approximate apparent Km values can nevertheless be read from the plotted curves, as stated below. Uncertainty therefore concerns both model and measurement. Useful discriminating tests, proposed rather than performed, include measuring initial rates across a matrix of substrate and inhibitor concentrations and fitting competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive and mixed models globally with confidence intervals; using reciprocal or Eadie-Hofstee displays only as diagnostics, since they distort error structure; checking reversibility by dilution or jump dilution; testing enzyme-concentration dependence to detect aggregation or tight binding, which would require a Morrison-type analysis; and measuring direct binding by isothermal titration calorimetry, surface plasmon resonance or microscale thermophoresis, ideally with a substrate-site mutant to test whether binding and catalysis compete. Competition with a known active-site ligand and structural data would further locate the site. The predictions differ: substrate competition and carefully validated mutations can constrain binding models, but allosteric coupling can also produce mutual exclusion and mutations need not affect every active-site inhibitor. Reporter replacement tests a different explanation: an apparent shift created by the detection system. the plot supports same Vmax with increased apparent Km, which identifies a competitive kinetic pattern under the assumed model, and it does not establish where the inhibitor binds. The plotted axes permit approximate Vmax of 100 nmol/min and apparent Km values near 20 and 60 micromolar to be read from the curves; these are synthetic model values, not estimated parameters with experimental uncertainty. A substrate-site mutation can alter folding or catalysis, so altered affinity needs independent protein-integrity controls.

## Strict second-round operational extension

The drawn asymptote is about 100 nmol/min; the half-maximal substrate positions are about 20 and 60 micromolar. These graphical model values describe a roughly threefold apparent-Km increase with unchanged Vmax, not experimentally estimated confidence intervals. Before validation, consult the assay's Methods, inhibitor handling and reporter protocol. Define vehicle/no-enzyme blanks, a known-product recovery control and a target-competent reference before data collection; use constant solvent and matched substrate handling.

For a proposed pilot, sample substrate at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 times the vehicle Km, and several inhibitor levels bounded by measured solubility. These are candidate settings to validate, not historical conditions. Convert the primary signal to nmol product; confirm a linear initial window with low fractional depletion and a detector calibration spanning every condition. Repeat product standards with inhibitor to detect absorption or quenching. If standards fail, use an independent product-separation assay rather than algebraically correcting an unvalidated optical signal. Report zero/weak signals as below the quantification limit and saturated measurements as invalid for parameter fitting.

Fit rates directly to competing kinetic models with a common error model, uncertainty and residual checks; do not decide from reciprocal-plot intersections alone. If confidence regions separate Vmax changes from Km changes, describe the compatible kinetic class; if the substrate range cannot identify Vmax, state that limitation. Jump dilution with an adequate observation period can test reversibility. Direct binding or structural assays require integrity controls, and a catalytic-site mutation can change folding or conformational coupling. Therefore altered binding after mutation or competition with substrate is not by itself physical-site localization. A preserved rightward shift in a direct product assay supports competitive kinetics; disappearance on reporter replacement supports interference; persistent ambiguity should remain a model-identification limit.
