# neu-k01: revised reference

Version: reference-v2.1. All procedures and numerical pilot criteria are proposed, not claims of historical execution. New revisions await owner review. Scientifically equivalent alternative designs can satisfy the rubric.

The recorded observation is that a slow fluorescent calcium indicator reports a larger response on trial B than on trial A, with acquisition and normalization described as comparable. The conclusion that follows without further calibration is the narrow one: the recorded indicator response is larger in trial B, while exact spike counts and timing remain uncertain. The alternative claim would require indicator amplitude to be a linear, calibrated count of spikes. Calcium-sensitive indicators produce an optical response influenced by calcium and the imaging/indicator system, which is a nonlinear and temporally filtered surrogate for spiking; a slow indicator integrates calcium transients, blurs burst structure, and can saturate or fail to resolve single spikes, so a larger amplitude does not map onto a proportionally larger spike number. The alternative claim misdescribes the measurement: fluorescence from a calcium-sensitive dye reports calcium, not membrane voltage. Voltage is measured with voltage-sensitive dyes, genetically encoded voltage indicators or electrophysiology. The alternative claim asserts field-wide synchrony, but a population fluorescence signal cannot establish that every neuron fired together; synchrony requires single-cell resolution plus explicit correlation or jitter measures, and a larger aggregate signal is compatible with many temporal patterns. Comparable acquisition and normalization help but are limited. Comparable acquisition constrains illumination, gain and frame timing, and normalization constrains how amplitudes are expressed, yet neither addresses dye loading or expression level per cell, baseline calcium, bleaching, compartment volume, or the nonlinear transfer from spikes to fluorescence, so cells or trials can still differ in effective indicator concentration.  Uncertainty therefore has two layers: biological uncertainty about how many spikes occurred and when, and measurement uncertainty about how fluorescence maps onto spiking. Useful discriminating tests, proposed rather than performed, include calibrating the indicator in situ with controlled calcium buffers or with ionomycin and EGTA to relate fluorescence to calcium concentration; simultaneous electrophysiology and imaging in the same cells to estimate the spike-to-fluorescence transfer function, including its saturating region; repeating the comparison with a faster indicator or with two-photon targeted recordings to resolve individual events; deconvolution with explicitly stated model assumptions and reported uncertainty; and reporting per-cell distributions of responses rather than one pooled amplitude. Each test has a different prediction. If paired ground-truth recordings show more spikes in B, calibrated imaging can test whether the optical response agrees within its measured operating range. A faster indicator does not guarantee single-event sensitivity or linearity. Calibration can reveal compression, unequal loading or baseline history. Saturation alone does not reverse a matched monotonic input-output relation; optical artifact tests remain necessary. the defensible statement is that the recorded calcium-indicator response was larger in trial B, and any spike count or timing claim requires calibration and event-resolved measurements before it can be asserted.

## Strict second-round operational extension

The immediate conclusion is optical: trial B has a larger recorded indicator response. It does not establish a larger free-calcium transient, calcium time integral, spike count or synchrony until relevant artifacts and response transformations are tested. Comparable acquisition does not fix cell motion, focus, baseline history or indicator expression. A monotonic saturating response can compress differences but does not, by saturation alone, reverse a matched monotonic input-output ordering.

Before validation, consult the indicator Methods and spike-calibration protocol for the relevant cell type and verify applicability. Define silent electrical periods as the negative event control, known electrophysiological spikes as the positive reference and a structural channel/indicator-negative preparation as optical artifact controls before recording. The structural channel can itself be motion sensitive; that sensitivity makes it useful for estimating displacement, not a guarantee that ratio correction removes artifacts.

Record synchronized somatic electrophysiology and imaging in the same cells. Pilot known spike trains spanning count, burst timing and inter-trial interval, including matched-count trains with different timing. Measure the camera clock offset and drift. Fix background/neuropil correction and F0 selection on training records, retain raw F, and report delta-F/F0 with baseline units and a warning when F0 approaches noise. Calibrate the noise floor, dynamic range and saturation region; use a predefined timing tolerance to match inferred and electrical events and report recall, precision, false events per second and count error with animal-clustered uncertainty. Test on independent cells or animals before generalizing.

If B's optical change occurs without an electrical change and follows measured axial motion or baseline history, the spike interpretation weakens; correlation with motion alone does not uniquely prove artifact. If independently measured spike differences predict the optical signal on held-out data, a calibrated claim becomes possible within that range. A faster indicator still requires validation and may trade sensitivity for speed. Failed ground-truth acquisition makes the spike claim unresolved; it must not be replaced by fluorescence-derived pseudo-ground-truth.
