# neu-k01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

The recorded observation is that a slow fluorescent calcium indicator reports a larger response on trial B than on trial A, with acquisition and normalization described as comparable. The conclusion that follows without further calibration is the narrow one: the recorded indicator response is larger in trial B, while exact spike counts and timing remain uncertain. The alternative claim would require indicator amplitude to be a linear, calibrated count of spikes. Calcium indicators report intracellular free calcium, which is a nonlinear and temporally filtered surrogate for spiking; a slow indicator integrates calcium transients, blurs burst structure, and can saturate or fail to resolve single spikes, so a larger amplitude does not map onto a proportionally larger spike number. The alternative claim misdescribes the measurement: fluorescence from a calcium-sensitive dye reports calcium, not membrane voltage. Voltage is measured with voltage-sensitive dyes, genetically encoded voltage indicators or electrophysiology. The alternative claim asserts field-wide synchrony, but a population fluorescence signal cannot establish that every neuron fired together; synchrony requires single-cell resolution plus explicit correlation or jitter measures, and a larger aggregate signal is compatible with many temporal patterns. Comparable acquisition and normalization help but are limited. Comparable acquisition constrains illumination, gain and frame timing, and normalization constrains how amplitudes are expressed, yet neither addresses dye loading or expression level per cell, baseline calcium, bleaching, compartment volume, or the nonlinear transfer from spikes to fluorescence, so cells or trials can still differ in effective indicator concentration. The case is also synthetic, and the supplied key is a draft under expert review, so this should be read as a teaching illustration rather than a laboratory result. Uncertainty therefore has two layers: biological uncertainty about how many spikes occurred and when, and measurement uncertainty about how fluorescence maps onto spiking. Useful discriminating tests, proposed rather than performed, include calibrating the indicator in situ with controlled calcium buffers or with ionomycin and EGTA to relate fluorescence to calcium concentration; simultaneous electrophysiology and imaging in the same cells to estimate the spike-to-fluorescence transfer function, including its saturating region; repeating the comparison with a faster indicator or with two-photon targeted recordings to resolve individual events; deconvolution with explicitly stated model assumptions and reported uncertainty; and reporting per-cell distributions of responses rather than one pooled amplitude. Each test has a different prediction. If trial B genuinely contains more spikes with similar timing, a faster indicator and calibrated imaging should show more resolved events and the transfer function should remain in its linear range. If the apparent difference is largely saturation, unequal loading or a few cells with unusually high signal, calibration should reveal a compressed transfer function or unequal effective dye concentration. the defensible statement is that the recorded calcium-indicator response was larger in trial B, and any spike count or timing claim requires calibration and event-resolved measurements before it can be asserted.
