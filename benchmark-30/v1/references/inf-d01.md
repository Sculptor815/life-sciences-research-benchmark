# inf-d01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

Proposed design. All analysis steps below are proposed; none has been executed. The goal is to identify a reproducible treatment-associated multi-omic program from paired tumor RNA, protein and metabolite measurements, and to explain the distinct roles of integration, enrichment and external validation. M1 states that thirty patients have pretreatment and post-treatment samples. M2 states that RNA and protein were run in different batches and that most post-treatment metabolomics samples share a batch. M3 states that some assays are missing. M4 states that a second small cohort exists and must remain untouched during development. The estimand should be defined at the patient level: the within-patient change from pretreatment to post-treatment, per assay, and the cross-assay program that summarizes those changes. Two goals must be separated: exploratory discovery of shared factors, and predictive or confirmatory validation in independent data. M2 contains the central design threat. If most post-treatment metabolomics samples come from one batch, treatment time point and metabolomics batch are largely or completely confounded. When confounding is complete, no amount of regression adjustment, batch correction, harmonization or multi-omic integration can recover the missing counterfactual, because there are no post-treatment samples measured in the other batch to separate the two effects. The honest options are to restrict metabolomics claims, to treat metabolomics as exploratory, to run a targeted balancing experiment in which paired pre- and post-treatment aliquots are re-measured across balanced batches with shared bridge samples, or to collect a new balanced set. Integration cannot fix a design problem. Observed evidence versus assumptions: the batch structure and missingness are reported facts; the assumption that a shared factor represents treatment biology rather than batch, donor or time is exactly what the analysis must test. Preparation, identity and quality control. Proposed steps: harmonize sample identifiers across all assays so that patient and time point are unambiguous; lock a sample key before analysis; record and verify batch identifiers for every assay; run assay-specific quality control, including RNA integrity, library metrics, protein yield and digest efficiency, chromatographic quality, internal standards and missingness patterns; prespecify quality-control thresholds and exclusion rules; and document clinical covariates such as treatment type, timing, tumor purity and response. Experimental unit, allocation and blinding. The independent unit is the patient, and each patient contributes a paired pre-post observation per assay. Splitting aliquots or samples from the same patient across development and validation partitions is prohibited, because it leaks patient-level information. Proposed allocation: cluster the split by patient, keep the external cohort locked until features and model choices are frozen, and blind the analyst to treatment labels during unsupervised factor discovery where feasible. Sample size should be calibrated: with thirty patients, estimate variance components for patient, batch and residual from a pilot or from technical replicates, then compute the detectable effect size for the prespecified factor-level contrast rather than assuming a power value. If the detectable effect is larger than the biologically plausible effect, the study is underpowered for that contrast, and this must be reported. Intervention and schedule. Sample collection times relative to treatment cycles must be specified by the author and kept consistent; any difference in the pre-post interval across patients is a covariate. Proposed analysis schedule: assay-specific normalization and quality control first; then missing-data handling; then integration; then enrichment; then locked external validation. Integration. Proposed approaches: a multi-view factor model or an explicitly justified late-integration strategy, with the patient-paired contrast as input and assay-specific preprocessing, rather than naive concatenation of all features. Report factor loadings per assay and quantify the association of each learned factor with batch, donor, treatment and clinical covariates. A factor whose variance is dominated by batch or donor structure should not be called a treatment program. Missing assays require an explicit missingness model; complete-case, available-case and imputation analyses should be compared as sensitivity analyses, with the assumption about missingness stated. Enrichment. Proposed approach: use the tested feature universe, meaning the genes, proteins and metabolites that were actually measured and passed quality control, with a dated gene-set or pathway release, and report effect direction and multiplicity. Enrichment against an inappropriate all-gene background can miscalibrate significance in either direction, and a small enrichment p-value does not establish causal direction or mechanism. External validation. Proposed approach: freeze preprocessing, feature selection, factor number and model form before touching the second cohort; then apply the locked pipeline to that cohort and evaluate whether the program reproduces in the same direction with a compatible effect size. Use patient-grouped resampling and nested selection rather than reusing validation data to tune the model. Decision rules: if a factor loses its association when batch-balanced subsets are analyzed, or tracks batch rather than time point, it is not established as a treatment program. If it reproduces in the locked cohort in the same direction, it becomes a candidate reproducible program subject to functional follow-up. If the metabolomics contrast is unidentifiable, report it as such rather than presenting a corrected estimate as though the design supported it. Troubleshooting. Proposed checks: if a factor is batch-dominated, restrict to balanced subsets, add a targeted balancing run, or drop the affected assay from confirmatory claims; if missingness is extensive and non-random, report bounds rather than a point estimate; if factors are unstable across resampling, reduce dimensionality and report stability metrics; if the external cohort is too small to test the full model, prespecify a single primary program-level test instead of many features. Limitations. The design is observational and pre-post, so it cannot separate treatment from time, disease progression or concurrent care; the sample size is small relative to feature count; metabolomics batch confounding may be complete and therefore unfixable computationally; the external cohort is small; and all parameter choices, timelines and effect sizes require author input.
