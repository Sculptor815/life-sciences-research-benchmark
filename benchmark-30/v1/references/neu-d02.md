# neu-d02: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

All experiments below are proposed; the source figure captions are not a protocol and no numeric values are reproduced. The design validates a calcium indicator as an activity readout by pairing it with electrophysiological ground truth and by controlling movement, focus, background and neuropil contamination.

Preparation and QC. Express the indicator in the target cell type and state, and include a structural reference channel in the same preparation. Before recording, verify cell health, baseline fluorescence, expression level and the absence of obvious movement or focus instability. If expression level varies, estimate abundance independently where feasible; calcium-dependent baseline brightness alone is not a pure expression measurement. Because exact indicator identity, expression level, imaging rate and analysis thresholds are unavailable, calibrate the fluorescence-to-spike relationship in the target cell type rather than importing values from another preparation. If a ground-truth electrode cannot be obtained for a particular cell, that cell cannot serve as a calibration unit for spike inference.

Experimental unit, allocation and blinding. The inferential unit for calibration is an independently recorded cell or an independent preparation, depending on the question; cells within one field are not independent replicates for the animal-level claim. Randomize visual-stimulus order and blocks where possible. Blind the analysis of fluorescence to the electrophysiological spike train and blind the spike detection to the fluorescence trace. Keep the allocation of stimulus conditions and analysis gates prespecified.

Handling and analysis sequence. Acquire simultaneous imaging and electrophysiology. Record spontaneous and evoked activity with known or independently detected spike times. Acquire the structural channel throughout. Apply three-dimensional displacement estimation and motion correction before fluorescence extraction. Extract somatic, dendritic and neuropil regions; compute neuropil-subtracted and non-subtracted traces as a sensitivity analysis. Calibrate the indicator with known spike counts across a range of firing rates, then test visual responses. The analysis sequence is: first verify alignment between imaging and electrophysiology; second correct motion; third extract regions and neuropil; fourth estimate spike inference on a training subset; fifth report detection rate, false-positive rate, temporal resolution and amplitude nonlinearity on a held-out subset; sixth compare visual-response detection with and without movement and neuropil controls; seventh test whether expression level changes the calibration.

Controls. Include a structural-reference-only analysis, a neuropil subtraction sensitivity analysis, a movement-corrected versus uncorrected comparison, and a no-stimulus or blank-stimulus control. If an indicator-negative or non-signalling control is available, use it to estimate background and movement artefacts. Use electrical ground truth as the independent benchmark. Include a known spike-count calibration set separate from the test set.

The primary quantitative contrast is the agreement between inferred activity and electrophysiological ground truth across the calibration range, reported as detection and false-positive measures, not merely correlation. The secondary contrast is the visual-response effect size with and without movement correction and neuropil subtraction. A third contrast is whether expression level or indicator kinetics shifts the inferred response at matched spike counts.

Time and dose calibration. Calibrate the imaging rate and analysis window to the indicator kinetics; if the indicator is slow or saturating, report the resulting temporal resolution rather than assuming spike resolution. Calibrate visual stimulus duration and inter-stimulus interval so that responses are separable from movement and from the previous stimulus. Calibrate spike-count ranges in the target cell type. If exact parameters are unavailable, run a pilot calibration and prespecify the accepted range.

Sample-size and power planning. Base power on independent cells or independent preparations for the calibration claim, and on independent animals for any animal-level claim. Estimate variance from a pilot; if unavailable, prespecify a calibration pilot and a minimum detectable change in detection or false-positive rate. Do not inflate n by treating multiple regions from one cell as independent.

Prespecified exclusions and stopping rules. Exclude cells with poor health, unstable baseline, failed motion correction, no ground-truth signal, or expression outside the calibrated range. Stop acquisition for technical timing drift or motion beyond calibrated recovery capacity. Retain saturation and inaccurate spike inference as performance failures, then delimit the validated operating range. Do not exclude cells because their inferred response disagrees with the hypothesis.

Missing data. Report missing electrophysiology, lost frames, or cells without ground truth. Do not impute spike times from fluorescence. For visual-response analyses, prespecify how trials with movement or lost tracking are handled.

Expected results. A positive validation would show that inferred activity tracks ground-truth spikes with acceptable detection and false-positive rates in the target cell type, that visual responses survive movement correction and neuropil subtraction, and that expression level does not dominate the calibration. A negative result would show poor detection, high false positives, or visual effects that disappear after controls. An ambiguous result would show good correlation but poor temporal resolution, or cell-type-specific calibration that does not generalize.

Artifact discrimination. The structural channel and three-dimensional motion estimation address movement and focus drift. Neuropil subtraction sensitivity addresses local contamination. Paired electrophysiology addresses inference error. Expression-level measurement addresses amplitude ambiguity. Stimulus blanks and movement controls address behaviour-related false signals.

Troubleshooting. If motion correction fails, improve the structural channel or stabilise the preparation. If neuropil subtraction removes the signal, quantify the contamination rather than assuming the signal is real. If spike inference is poor, recalibrate in the target cell type or lower the temporal claim. If expression is too high, reduce expression and repeat the calibration.

Conditional conclusion. The data support calcium imaging as an activity readout only within the calibrated cell type, state and expression range, and only for the temporal resolution demonstrated against ground truth. They do not establish that fluorescence equals instantaneous spike output universally. If movement, neuropil or expression confounds remain, the conclusion is limited to a correlative visual response, not a spike-level inference.

Curator clarification and operational extension. The primary calibration is somatic fluorescence against simultaneously recorded somatic spikes. A somatic electrode is not ground truth for local dendritic-spine calcium or synaptic input; a spine-specific claim requires its own validation. Acquire a structural z-stack or volumetric reference before the recording, track reference-plane displacement, and delimit the range over which axial correction is validated. A lone two-dimensional channel cannot reconstruct arbitrary three-dimensional motion. Synchronize acquisition with shared trigger pulses, quantify clock drift, and measure dropped frames. Fix ROI masks and neuropil exclusion rings without consulting test spikes. Fit neuropil coefficients, baseline correction and spike-inference thresholds only on training preparations. Match inferred and electrical events within a preregistered timing tolerance; report precision, recall, false events per recording time, temporal error and confidence intervals clustered by animal. Include silent periods and high-rate trains to assess false positives and saturation. Do not omit saturated or poorly inferred cells simply to improve performance. A calibration that succeeds in held-out trials from the same cell supports a narrower claim than success in held-out animals.
