# mol-p01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

Source identity and claim support are separate judgments, and this item turns on keeping them separate. On source identity, the submitted citation C1 names I. Vale, 'Native A-B recognition', 2024, record LS-M17. The registry entry C2 for the same record names J. Reed, 'Tagged protein association', 2022. Author, title and year all disagree. The appropriate classification is a citation mismatch: the supplied reference does not match the record it points to. It does not prove that any article is nonexistent, and it does not prove fabrication. The snapshot records no correction or retraction for LS-M17 as of the stated retrieval date, and the absence of a notice in one snapshot is evidence only about this dataset at this time, not about universal absence. The dossier states that identifiers refer only to this teaching dossier, so nothing here should be read as an accusation about real people. On the claim that A directly binds B in unmodified cells, the supplied evidence does not support it. E1 reports that tagged A and B were overexpressed and co-immunoprecipitated in three independent cultures, with input and IgG controls. That supports an association between the tagged proteins under the tested overexpression conditions. Input controls show the proteins are present in the lysate, and IgG controls address nonspecific capture by the antibody backbone, so E1 is more informative than a bare co-immunoprecipitation. But co-immunoprecipitation detects co-complex formation, not direct molecular contact: A and B could be bridged by a third protein, by nucleic acid, or by residence in a shared larger complex. Overexpression also changes stoichiometry and can create interactions that do not occur at endogenous levels, and epitope tags can themselves contribute to association. Three independent cultures support replication of the same manipulated condition; they do not establish direct contact or native regulation. E2 states that no endogenous co-immunoprecipitation and no purified-protein binding experiment are supplied, which is exactly where the missing evidence would have to come from. So the claim carries two unsupported components at once: 'directly' requires a molecular-contact measurement, and 'unmodified cells' requires endogenous, tag-free expression. Several incorrect alternatives recur. One is to treat the registry mismatch as proof of fabrication; a metadata mismatch is a sourcing problem, not a scientific verdict, and it cannot be escalated into a misconduct claim. Another is to treat three cultures as three independent demonstrations of direct binding; replicating a condition does not upgrade the inferential reach of the method. A third is to treat the IgG control as excluding every indirect bridging protein; IgG controls address antibody-specific artifacts, not the existence of a bridge. A fourth is to declare the claim supported simply because A and B co-immunoprecipitate. Uncertainty remains: the mismatch could reflect a transcription error, an alternate version, a preprint, or a genuinely different work, and this dossier cannot resolve which. Discriminating tests, proposed rather than performed, include endogenous co-immunoprecipitation with validated antibodies plus a reciprocal pull-down; nuclease and high-salt washes to probe nucleic-acid or weak bridges; proximity-dependent labeling or FRET at endogenous expression; purified recombinant binding with an affinity measurement and competition; and disruption of candidate bridging proteins. A tagged system can still be used, but only alongside an untagged, endogenous test with tag and expression controls. If direct binding is real, purified proteins should interact with a defined affinity and the endogenous interaction should survive bridge disruption; if the signal reflects a shared complex or an overexpression artifact, those tests should separate the claim from the data. Until then the defensible statement is an association of tagged A and B under the tested conditions, with identity and notice questions still open.
