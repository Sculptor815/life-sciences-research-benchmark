The plotted initial-rate curves follow ideal Michaelis-Menten kinetics, and the comparison of inhibitor with vehicle shows the same maximal rate but a rightward shift, so the best description is same Vmax with increased apparent Km. In the simplest scheme this is the signature of substrate-competitive inhibition: the inhibitor raises the substrate concentration required for half-maximal velocity by a factor of one plus inhibitor concentration over Ki, while saturating substrate can outcompete it and restore Vmax. Choice A, lower Vmax with unchanged Km, describes a pattern in which saturation cannot overcome the inhibition, as in noncompetitive or irreversible inhibition, and it does not match shared asymptotes. Choice C, higher Vmax and lower Km, describes activation rather than inhibition. The alternative claim is the important conceptual error: a competitive kinetic pattern does not identify the physical inhibitor binding site. Kinetics reports how rate depends on substrate and inhibitor concentrations under an assumed model; it does not report where a molecule binds. An inhibitor could produce a competitive pattern by binding the catalytic site, but it could also bind an allosteric site that changes substrate affinity, or interfere with a coupled or reporter step in a way that mimics competition. Distinguishing those possibilities requires binding and structural evidence, not another curve shape. The case is synthetic and the supplied key is a draft under expert review, so the plot illustrates ideal behavior. Its assumptions matter: idealized curves exclude substrate inhibition, cooperativity, slow-binding behavior, tight binding when inhibitor and enzyme concentrations are comparable, and assay artifacts such as compound absorbance or quenching. The inhibitor concentration, Ki and experimental fit uncertainty are not supplied. Approximate apparent Km values can nevertheless be read from the plotted curves, as stated below. Uncertainty therefore concerns both model and measurement. Useful discriminating tests, proposed rather than performed, include measuring initial rates across a matrix of substrate and inhibitor concentrations and fitting competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive and mixed models globally with confidence intervals; using reciprocal or Eadie-Hofstee displays only as diagnostics, since they distort error structure; checking reversibility by dilution or jump dilution; testing enzyme-concentration dependence to detect aggregation or tight binding, which would require a Morrison-type analysis; and measuring direct binding by isothermal titration calorimetry, surface plasmon resonance or microscale thermophoresis, ideally with a substrate-site mutant to test whether binding and catalysis compete. Competition with a known active-site ligand and structural data would further locate the site. The predictions differ: if inhibition is truly substrate-competitive at the active site, direct binding should be displaced by substrate and a catalytic-site mutation should change inhibitor affinity; if the pattern is allosteric or reporter-based, binding may persist without substrate competition, or the apparent shift may change when the coupled reporter is replaced by a direct product measurement. the plot supports same Vmax with increased apparent Km, which identifies a competitive kinetic pattern under the assumed model, and it does not establish where the inhibitor binds. The plotted axes permit approximate Vmax of 100 nmol/min and apparent Km values near 20 and 60 micromolar to be read from the curves; these are synthetic model values, not estimated parameters with experimental uncertainty. A substrate-site mutation can alter folding or catalysis, so altered affinity needs independent protein-integrity controls.
