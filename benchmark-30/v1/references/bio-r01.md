# bio-r01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

A decisive next question is whether isolated F1 can use externally supplied mechanical work to synthesize ATP when its rotor is driven in the reverse direction. The original observation establishes movement associated with ATP consumption, but it does not demonstrate chemical synthesis caused by mechanical control. The key hypothesis is reversibility through a particular mechanical coordinate. A competing explanation is that forced movement produces an optical or chemical artifact without reversing catalysis. Another is that ATP detected after manipulation was present initially, released from a bound pool, or generated by an unrelated contaminating enzyme.

Stage one defines what would count as synthesis. The primary endpoint is newly available ATP attributable to controlled mechanical input, measured relative to matched starting material and background. A transient release of a prebound nucleotide is insufficient to establish repeated catalytic synthesis. A direction-dependent chemical output should persist over multiple turnovers and depend on both the enzyme and the necessary substrates. The proposed conclusion concerns isolated F1 under defined conditions; it is not automatically a measurement of the complete membrane ATP synthase or physiological proton coupling.

Stage two creates a material inventory. Document the protein construct, subunit composition, purification batches, attachment chemistry, substrate lots, storage conditions, and all reagents used in ATP detection. Independently verify enzyme identity and catalytic competence before attaching the mechanical handle. Measure ATP contamination in ADP, buffer, surfaces, and the reporter mixture. Include a procedure for monitoring background over the same time scale as the main experiment. If purification changes substrate composition or removes stabilizing ions, remeasure the relevant concentrations rather than assuming the starting recipe remains valid.

Stage three constructs a controlled mechanical interface. Anchor the stator through a defined attachment and connect a manipulable marker to the rotor, while minimizing alternative linkages. Verify that the observed marker corresponds to the intended complex and not an aggregate or nonspecific surface attachment. Record the fraction of candidate objects that are trackable, coupled, stationary, detached, or ambiguous. Do not select only impressive rotating objects after the reaction outcome is known. The coupling geometry must permit interpretation of direction relative to the protein rather than merely clockwise motion in a camera image.

Stage four establishes the direction convention empirically. In a separate validation condition, observe fuel-driven rotation in the preparation and define its viewing orientation. Record camera handedness, image flips, surface orientation, and any change introduced by the acquisition software. Assign the reverse direction relative to that verified reference. If enzymes can attach in more than one orientation, quantify or exclude the ambiguous geometry through a prespecified rule. A command labeled clockwise is not meaningful unless its relationship to the enzyme's hydrolysis direction has been established.

Stage five calibrates the mechanical actuator. Measure how applied commands translate into actual marker movement across the intended operating range. Determine whether the rotor follows continuously, slips, stalls, or detaches. Estimate the relevant load and the stability of the applied field or trap, with a documented uncertainty model. Record actual trajectories during chemical measurement rather than substituting the actuator's nominal frequency. If mechanical work is used to calculate efficiency, the calibration must support an energy estimate; angular displacement alone does not determine work without a torque model or measurement.

Stage six calibrates ATP detection in the complete reaction matrix. Prepare standards and spike-recovery samples spanning the expected range, including the relevant substrates, protein, marker particles, and device conditions. Test linearity, dynamic range, response time, photometric or fluorescence background, and signal persistence after an ATP pulse. If the detector consumes ATP, model that consumption or use a sampling design that permits product estimation. A reporter response can lag behind production, so simple differences between consecutive intervals may confuse carryover with new synthesis. An orthogonal nucleotide measurement provides an additional check where sensitivity permits.

Stage seven specifies the main factorial comparison. Include reverse motion, forward motion, and no imposed motion, crossed with active enzyme and an appropriate inactive or uncoupled control. Include substrate-omission conditions and device exposure without a properly coupled enzyme. Match temperature, illumination, mechanical-marker concentration, timing, and reagent composition as closely as possible. The inactive control must not introduce a new chemical interference into the ATP assay. Explain which contrast addresses directionality, which addresses substrate dependence, and which excludes the actuator or reporter as the source of the signal.

Stage eight executes a time-resolved sequence under a frozen schedule. Start with a baseline measurement, apply the assigned mechanical interval, and follow the chemical signal after the interval. Counterbalance direction order or use independent chambers when previous rotation can alter the enzyme or reaction mixture. Keep a shared clock for actuator commands, trajectory acquisition, and chemical detection. Record deviations such as focus loss, evaporation, chamber leakage, device interruption, or particle detachment as they occur. Do not reclassify these events after inspecting whether a chamber supports the hypothesis.

Stage nine examines enzyme integrity and reversibility of the manipulation itself. After the mechanical experiment, test whether the preparation can still perform its established catalytic operation in a separately justified validation condition. Compare before and after behavior where the assay permits, and distinguish irreversible mechanical damage from a genuine lack of reverse coupling. An aggressive force that destroys the construct cannot meaningfully test the native catalytic relationship. Conversely, preserving a familiar movement pattern is supportive but does not by itself prove that every active site remained intact.

Stage ten specifies experimental units and uncertainty. Independent protein preparations and independently assembled reaction chambers provide different levels of replication. Many trajectories within one chamber do not create many independent chemical synthesis experiments if all share the same reagent pool and readout. Use a hierarchical analysis or chamber-level summaries appropriate to the data structure. Prespecify the minimum product difference that would be scientifically meaningful and justify the number of independent preparations needed to estimate it. Retain raw traces, calibration files, failed chambers, and the reasons for each excluded interval.

Stage eleven evaluates the complete result pattern. A credible result requires a product increase that depends on the correct mechanical direction, functional coupling, active enzyme, and synthesis substrates. The measured rotor must actually follow the imposed trajectory during the relevant interval. ATP standards must behave consistently across conditions, and background correction must not manufacture the effect. If signal appears equally with inactive protein or absent substrates, chemical contamination or detection interference remains likely. If apparent synthesis appears only after a large mechanical disturbance, release of a bound pool or surface material remains unresolved.

Stage twelve treats efficiency as a separate claim with additional requirements. Estimating ATP per turn requires the number of productively coupled enzymes, their actual rotations, the recovery of chemical product, and the reporter's consumption or loss. Estimating energetic efficiency additionally requires mechanical work and chemical free-energy accounting under the actual reaction composition. Unknown inactive fractions or mixed orientation can distort these estimates. Therefore, evidence for reverse operation can be convincing even when exact coupling stoichiometry remains uncertain. Report the narrower successful inference rather than converting a qualitative effect into unsupported perfect efficiency.

A productive null result is also interpretable when the assay is demonstrably sensitive and the mechanical connection works. It can constrain reverse operation within the tested load, substrate, and timing range. It cannot exclude every alternative actuator geometry or operating condition. If the detection limit is too high to observe the predicted output, the experiment is inconclusive, not a refutation. The same distinction applies when the active fraction is unknown. A rigorous answer identifies these dependencies before collecting data and gives decision rules for successful, null, technically failed, and ambiguous outcomes.

Finally, preserve the distinction between a historical discovery target and the proposed implementation. The causal direction of the hypothesis, external-work intervention, substrate-defined chemical measurement, and discriminatory controls determine whether this answer matches the subsequent research direction. Exact equipment settings should come from verified primary methods, their supplements, and current calibration; unverified numbers must remain explicit gaps. None of the additional controls proposed here should be represented as an accusation that the original experiment lacked them. The purpose is to design a transparent test whose full evidence chain could be audited independently.
