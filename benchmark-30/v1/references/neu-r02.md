# neu-r02: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

The useful biological question is whether early patterned visual experience helps establish the distribution of cortical responsiveness to the two eyes. Adult organization alone cannot distinguish an experience-independent developmental program from experience-dependent modification. A discriminating proposal compares controlled alteration of one eye's visual experience with matched rearing controls and tests each eye independently after optical access is restored. The endpoints are cortical response distributions and competing peripheral explanations, not an assumption that all reduced responses reflect cortical rewiring.

Before work, justify the model and developmental stage using available evidence and obtain an approved welfare-reviewed protocol with veterinary endpoints. Do not reconstruct historical deprivation procedures from an abstract. Specify the approved reversible manipulation, monitoring schedule and stopping criteria from that protocol. Consider whether existing data or a less invasive validated manipulation can answer the question. The packet does not supply animal numbers, exposure duration, anesthesia, recording coordinates or optical parameters; these must be justified and documented before execution.

Allocate independent animals within litter and developmental-stage blocks to altered visual experience, sham handling and normal experience, balancing sex or other justified covariates. Counterbalance which eye is affected. A bilateral-experience-control arm may help separate interocular competition from general visual deprivation, but its interpretation and welfare cost require a specific justification. Animals, not recorded neurons, are independent treatment units. Randomize recording order and maintain blinded group codes for stimulus presentation, unit selection and analysis.

Document baseline ocular health and the effectiveness of the experience manipulation using independent observations. Monitor optical clarity and general development throughout. At the prespecified endpoint, verify that both eyes can transmit the test stimulus comparably; persistent obstruction cannot be called cortical plasticity. Record refraction or alignment where relevant and control illumination, contrast and retinal stimulus position. Use independent retinal or visual-pathway measurements to evaluate peripheral function when the cortical responses differ. A normal-looking eye does not by itself prove normal retinal signaling.

Map receptive fields before presenting a randomized, counterbalanced stimulus set to each eye separately. Match stimulus luminance, contrast, orientation coverage and timing across eyes. Include blank trials, repeated stimuli and eye-isolation controls to prevent unintended stimulation of the other eye. Record neural signals using fixed depth/position sampling rules that do not depend on finding a desired ocular preference. Establish unit isolation, response reliability and baseline stability criteria before decoding treatment labels. Retain neurons without a visually evoked response in a separately reported category rather than silently deleting them.

For each qualified unit, quantify baseline-corrected responses from each eye, orientation tuning and reliability with uncertainty. Define a bounded ocular-dominance index only when its response denominator is sufficiently measurable; weak responses need an explicit category rather than an unstable ratio. Report distributions per animal and the proportions of contralateral, ipsilateral, binocular and unresponsive units. Use a hierarchical model or animal-level distributional contrast, with sampling location as a documented nuisance variable. Do not let animals with many recorded neurons dominate the treatment estimate.

Prespecify a primary shift in the ocular-dominance distribution and a meaningful animal-level effect. Use pilot between-animal variability to plan power or precision. Record litter, developmental stage, recording site and all technical losses. Blinded technical exclusions may address recording instability or failed stimulus isolation; an animal with a weak deprivation effect must remain an outcome. Report deviations from the approved protocol. If peripheral deficits prevent an equivalent retinal stimulus, the localization question is unresolved even when the cortical group contrast is large.

A systematic shift toward the experienced eye, with validated input to both eyes and controlled cortical sampling, supports experience-dependent organization. It does not by itself identify a synaptic site, eliminate thalamic contributions or establish the full developmental sensitive period. To localize further, compare cortical effects with upstream physiological measurements. To test timing or recovery, run separately powered developmental-onset or restoration cohorts; neither can be inferred from one endpoint. A null group contrast may reflect insufficient manipulation or low precision and should be interpreted with manipulation checks and intervals rather than as proof of an innate immutable circuit.

The sealed Wiesel and Hubel follow-up measured cortical responses after monocular visual deprivation. A qualified historical-direction match requires the developmental-experience question, controlled unilateral manipulation, eye-specific cortical recording and the predicted relative ocular-response change with peripheral and sampling controls. Merely proposing more receptive-field recordings or naming a famous critical period does not qualify. Equivalent approved designs can receive quality credit. This is a proposed modern audit of causal logic, not a claim that all historical animal procedures or all supporting methods have been reviewed.
