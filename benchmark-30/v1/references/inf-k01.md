# inf-k01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

The item reports a well-controlled genome-wide association result: a lead noncoding SNP near gene G is associated with the trait, and several variants in linkage disequilibrium span both G and H. No functional or colocalization data are supplied. The justified conclusion is the locus is associated, and identifying the causal variant and the effector gene requires further evidence. The claim that G is necessarily causal because it is closest, confuses physical proximity with mechanism. Noncoding variants can act on distant genes through chromatin contacts, enhancers and promoters, and the nearest transcript need not be the target. The claim that the lead SNP must be the only causal variant, ignores linkage disequilibrium. The lead SNP is the most strongly associated marker in the sample, but it is a statistical marker for a haplotype; several correlated variants can carry the same association signal, and any of them, or a combination, could be causal. The claim that whole-genome sequencing alone proves the mechanism, confuses variant discovery with functional evidence. Sequencing can find and genotype variants; it cannot show which one changes a regulatory element or which gene is affected. The key principle is that genetic association through linkage disequilibrium is not causality. Three claims should be kept distinct: the locus is associated; a specific causal variant exists and can be nominated; and a specific effector gene and mechanism explain the association. The supplied evidence supports only the first. Uncertainty is substantial. The lead SNP may be a proxy whose true causal variant is badly genotyped or imputed. Fine-mapping depends on the reference panel matching the study population, and mismatch can misplace credible sets. Multiple independent signals may exist at the locus, and conditional analysis is needed to separate them. Ancestry-specific linkage disequilibrium means a signal that maps one way in one population may map differently in another, and winner's curse can inflate the lead effect. Useful discriminating analyses, proposed rather than performed, include conditional association analysis to test for independent signals; statistical fine-mapping with a matched reference panel to produce credible sets; colocalization between the trait association and expression or protein quantitative trait loci in relevant tissues; functional annotation of credible variants, including chromatin state, transcription-factor motifs and allele-specific reporter assays; chromatin conformation data to link candidate elements to promoters; and, most directly, perturbation of the candidate variant in an isogenic system using base editing or CRISPR with appropriate controls. Replication in an independent, ancestry-appropriate cohort strengthens any nominated variant. Each test has a distinct prediction. If a single variant drives the signal, conditioning on the correctly measured causal variant should remove that signal under a correctly specified model; strong linkage can still leave a broad credible set. If the effector is G, colocalization and perturbation of a G-linked regulatory element should track the association. If the effector is H or another distal gene, those same tests should point elsewhere. the supplied evidence establishes a locus association, the linked variants prevent assigning causality to the lead SNP, and G cannot be declared the causal gene from distance or from sequencing alone.
