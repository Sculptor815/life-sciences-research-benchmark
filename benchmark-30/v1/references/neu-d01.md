# neu-d01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

Proposed design. All procedures below are proposed; none has been performed. The question is whether projection P to Q is necessary during cue retrieval in a learned choice task, while addressing movement and sensory confounds. O1 states that P projects to Q and to another region, so P is not a single-target population; O2 states that an inhibitory opsin can be targeted to P neurons projecting to Q; O3 states that correct choices are the proposed outcome but the task requires locomotion; O4 states that light delivery can be visible to the animal. Necessity here means that suppressing the P-to-Q projection during the retrieval window reduces correct performance under conditions where alternative explanations are controlled. It does not mean that the projection stores the memory, and it does not establish sufficiency. Observed evidence versus assumptions: O1 through O4 are design constraints, not results. The assumption that the targeted opsin reaches only Q-projecting P neurons requires verification, because soma-targeted inhibition of P would suppress all P outputs, including the collateral projection to the other region, and would therefore not isolate P-to-Q. Terminal inhibition in Q is more projection-selective but can affect fibers of passage and can change local Q excitability through presynaptic release, so both strategies carry different confounds and the choice must be prespecified. Preparation, identity and quality control. Proposed steps: define the P and Q region identities and coordinates from the author's atlas and prior tracing, since coordinates cannot be assumed; verify opsin expression and targeting with histology in every animal or in a reported subset, quantifying overlap between opsin-positive somata and retrogradely labeled Q-projecting neurons; verify physiological suppression with ex vivo or in vivo recordings showing reduced activity under the light protocol; confirm that the viral strategy does not label neighboring structures; and confirm behavioral competence, including cue detection and reward consumption, before the main sessions. Experimental unit, allocation and blinding. The independent unit is the animal. Trials and sessions are repeated measures within animals. Proposed allocation: counterbalance light and no-light trials within session in a randomized interleaved schedule, counterbalance session order across animals, blind the experimenter and the outcome coder to condition, and prespecify the primary contrast as accuracy during the retrieval window. Sample size should be calibrated rather than assumed: run a pilot with a small number of animals, estimate the variance of the animal-level accuracy difference, and compute the number of animals required for 80 percent power at a two-sided 0.05 level for the prespecified within-animal contrast; if the pilot variance makes the design infeasible, report that limit instead of asserting a number. Intervention and schedule. The opsin, promoter, viral titer, light wavelength, power, pulse pattern and duration are unknown and must come from the author. Proposed calibration first: in the targeted preparation, measure the minimum light power that produces reliable suppression in the physiological assay, using a power meter at the fiber tip and monitoring for heating and tissue damage, then use the lowest effective power and a matched control protocol in behavior. Proposed schedule: habituate animals to the tether and to light delivery without opsin before training; train to a prespecified performance criterion; then run test sessions with light delivered only during the retrieval window, with the window defined behaviorally by cue onset and choice initiation and reported in milliseconds. Controls. Proposed controls include opsin-negative lit littermates receiving identical light; within-animal no-light trials interleaved in the same session; light-only trials in the absence of the cue to test whether light itself is a usable cue given O4; a control opsin or fluorophore-only group; a group with opsin in a non-targeted population to test anatomical specificity; and counterbalanced light delivery so that light is not confounded with a fixed side, trial position or reward schedule. Because O4 warns that light can be visible, shielding the fiber, using a longer-wavelength opsin, or explicitly measuring light detection in a separate task are proposed mitigations. Ordered measurements. Proposed order: expression and targeting verification; physiological suppression verification; light-cue detection check; habituation sessions; training to criterion; test sessions with interleaved light and no-light trials; concurrent locomotion measurement, including speed, distance and trial-initiation latency; cue detection and reward-consumption measures; and, if deficits appear, an independent low-motor-demand variant of the task. Analysis and decision rules. Analyze trial-level accuracy with a mixed-effects logistic model including light condition as a within-animal factor and animal-level random intercepts, with planned contrasts and confidence intervals; analyze reaction time and locomotion as secondary outcomes; report animal-level effect estimates. Decision rules: a selective retrieval-window deficit in accuracy with preserved locomotion, cue detection and reward consumption, absent in opsin-negative lit controls and absent in light-only trials, supports necessity under the tested conditions. If performance falls together with movement or cue detection, the deficit cannot be attributed to retrieval, and the session or task must be redesigned or a mediation analysis with explicit assumptions used. If no effect appears, check opsin expression, targeting, light power and suppression efficacy before concluding that the projection is unnecessary; a null result with unverified suppression is inconclusive. Troubleshooting. Proposed checks: if suppression fails physiologically, retarget or increase expression and recalibrate power; if movement is reduced, lower light power, shorten the pulse, or use terminal inhibition with the caveats noted; if animals appear to detect the light, shield delivery or switch opsin; if seizures or heating occur, reduce duty cycle and use a red-shifted opsin; if performance is at ceiling or floor, adjust task difficulty before the main test. Limitations. Necessity in a retrieval window does not establish sufficiency, does not show where a memory is stored, and does not exclude a purely performance-related role; P has a collateral projection, so projection specificity depends entirely on the targeting strategy; terminal inhibition has its own confounds; and all parameter values require author input or calibration. Human review remains pending. Operational clarification: expression and suppression checks before behavior use a separate pilot or nonterminal recordings; verify anatomical spread in the actual study animals by postmortem histology after testing. Terminal illumination does not automatically isolate synaptic transmission: validate axonal and local physiological effects, collateral recruitment and possible rebound responses with the chosen inhibitory tool. Red-shifting or lowering power does not by itself eliminate visual detection or heating, so repeat matched control measurements. Any parameter change prompted by a failed pilot must be frozen before the confirmatory phase; do not retune it selectively after observing the primary behavioral result. Distinguish motor mediation from a parallel effect and do not assume conditioning on post-treatment speed identifies a causal retrieval effect.
