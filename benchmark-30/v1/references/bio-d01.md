# bio-d01: first-round reference

This is the unchanged first-round reference text. The owner confirmed careful review on 3 October 2026. Earlier pending-review statements inside the historical text are retained as part of the snapshot, not current metadata. Corrections and expanded execution requirements appear in version two.

Proposed design. Every experiment below is proposed and none has been performed. The question is what a compound that lowers a coupled fluorescence signal actually does: inhibit the target enzyme directly, compete with or otherwise limit substrate availability, or interfere with the reporter system that generates the signal. D1, D2 and D3 shape the design. D1 says a single substrate concentration and a 30-minute endpoint were used, so the existing observation cannot distinguish a change in maximal rate from a change in apparent substrate affinity; a single point on a progress curve conflates rate, depletion and lag. D2 says product detection uses a second enzyme and a fluorophore, which introduces an additional enzyme the compound could inhibit and a coupling step that could become rate-limiting. D3 says the compound absorbs near the excitation wavelength, which is a direct mechanism for a false fluorescence decrease through inner-filter absorption; at high concentration the compound may also quench emission or react with the fluorophore. Purified target and reporter enzymes are available, which makes an orthogonal cell-free design feasible. Observed evidence versus assumptions: the only observed fact is that the coupled fluorescence signal is lower with compound than without. Everything else, including target inhibition, is an interpretive hypothesis. Three main hypotheses follow. First, direct target inhibition, which can be competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive or mixed with respect to substrate. Second, substrate-related effects, such as the compound consuming, sequestering or chemically modifying the substrate, or acting as an alternative substrate. Third, reporter interference, including inner-filter absorption, static or dynamic quenching, inhibition of the coupling enzyme, product sequestration or altered fluorophore chemistry. Preparation, identity and quality control. Proposed steps: confirm target and reporter enzyme identity and purity, for example by mass spectrometry and activity assays; determine the substrate Km and the reporter enzyme Km for the product under assay conditions; verify that the coupling enzyme is in excess and not rate-limiting by adding extra reporter and checking that the observed rate is unchanged; establish product standard curves and fluorescence linearity, including quench and recovery checks; and characterize the compound itself for identity, purity, solubility and aggregation by nephelometry or turbidity, recording its absorbance and emission spectra under assay conditions. Experimental unit, allocation and blinding. The independent unit is an independent enzyme preparation or an independent assay run on a separate day, with wells as technical replicates. Proposed allocation: randomize well positions and plate order, include vehicle and a reference inhibitor on each plate, and blind the analyst to compound identity and concentration during reading and analysis. Sample size should come from a prespecified calibration: run a pilot with independent runs to estimate the residual variance of initial rates, then choose the number of runs needed to estimate Km, Vmax and Ki with acceptably narrow confidence intervals, rather than assuming a fixed effect size. Intervention and schedule. Proposed calibration for concentrations: first determine the compound's solubility limit and its absorbance at the assay's excitation and emission wavelengths, and define a working range below the solubility limit and below a prespecified optical interference threshold. If absorbance at the excitation wavelength is high enough to distort the signal, switch primary detection to an orthogonal direct product measurement and keep fluorescence only as a secondary readout. Proposed schedule: measure product formation over time at several early time points to establish initial-rate conditions for each condition rather than relying on a single 30-minute endpoint, and measure the compound dose-response at each substrate concentration. Controls. Proposed controls include vehicle; no-target controls to detect any signal generated without target enzyme; reporter-only reactions with known product to test reporter inhibition and product recovery; compound-only wells for background fluorescence; a fluorescence standard read with and without compound to quantify inner-filter and quenching effects; a known target inhibitor as a positive control; a known reporter-enzyme inhibitor as a positive control for reporter interference; and aggregation controls such as detergent or bovine serum albumin addition plus enzyme-concentration dependence. Ordered measurements. Proposed order: compound characterization first; then reporter and coupling quality control; then direct product measurement; then initial-rate time courses; then the substrate and inhibitor matrix; then reversibility and binding assays. Analysis and decision rules. Fit competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive and mixed models globally to the matrix with confidence intervals, and compare them using prespecified criteria and residual diagnostics; do not select a model by the highest R-squared from one endpoint. Report product recovery in the reporter-only control. Decision rules: if the compound inhibits the reporter-only reaction or if product recovery falls with compound, the fluorescence decrease is not interpretable as target inhibition and the direct readout must carry the analysis. If direct measurement shows no change in product while fluorescence falls, reporter interference is established. If direct measurement shows true target inhibition with unchanged Vmax and increased apparent Km, a substrate-competitive pattern is supported under the assumed model, but this does not identify the physical binding site: it requires direct binding experiments and active-site competition tests, and allosteric mechanisms can also produce competitive kinetics. If Vmax falls with unchanged Km, or behavior is time dependent, consider noncompetitive, irreversible or aggregation-based inhibition, and test reversibility by dilution or jump dilution; if the inhibitor concentration approaches the enzyme concentration, use a tight-binding analysis. Troubleshooting. Proposed checks: if the rate is not linear over time, reduce enzyme loading and shorten the read window; reducing substrate can worsen fractional depletion; if substrate depletion is detected, lower enzyme concentration or measure earlier; if the compound precipitates, reduce concentration or add a solubilizing agent consistently across conditions; if results depend on the reporter enzyme batch, standardize lots and retest. Limitations. The design is in vitro and cannot establish cellular target engagement or selectivity; coupled assays remain vulnerable to coupling artifacts even with controls; the compound's identity and potency are unknown here, so concentrations and Ki cannot be stated in advance; and the kinetic model is an interpretation under assumptions rather than a physical binding measurement.
