# Proposed protocol: test for a heat-stable, separable fermentative cofactor activity in yeast juice

**Status: all experiments below are proposed.** No chemical identity, molecular structure, or NAD identity is assumed or claimed.

## Recommended decision rule

A heat-stable separable factor would be supported **only if** an enzyme-containing retentate regains fermentative activity when recombined with a matched low-molecular-weight fraction or boiled extract, while the following are all controlled or fail to reproduce rescue: pH, inorganic ions, phosphate, volume/dilution, total protein, nonspecific protein stabilization, and measurable enzyme damage. The result should also reproduce after an independent, orthogonal separation.

The resulting conclusion would be operational:

> Under the tested yeast-juice conditions, restoration of fermentation requires a separable, heat-stable activity present in the low-molecular-weight fraction or boiled extract and absent from the enzyme-containing retentate.

This would **not** establish that the activity is one chemical species, identify its structure, or show that it is NAD.

---

# 1. Scientific logic

## Evidence → inference → testable conclusion

| Evidence in packet | Permitted inference | Proposed test |
|---|---|---|
| Yeast-juice filtrate and retentate were individually inactive but regained activity when combined. | Activity may require at least two separable components: an enzyme-containing component and a diffusible/filtrable complement. | Measure fermentation in retentate, filtrate, and their recombination at matched final volume and composition. |
| Boiled extract can compensate. | The complementary activity may survive boiling and therefore be operationally heat-stable. | Compare rescue by unheated filtrate, heat-treated filtrate, and boiled extract. |
| Separation may alter pH, salts, phosphate, enzyme integrity, or dilution. | Apparent “factor” rescue could instead arise from matrix correction or nonspecific stabilization. | Standardize buffer, ions, phosphate, volume, protein, and enzyme integrity across all mixtures. |
| Compensation does not identify chemical structure or NAD. | Functional complementation alone is insufficient for molecular identification. | Restrict conclusion to a heat-stable separable activity. Do not infer NAD identity. |

# 2. Experimental overview

Each independent yeast-juice preparation will be divided into matched aliquots and processed in parallel.

1. Establish that unfractionated yeast juice ferments reproducibly.
2. Fractionate one aliquot into an enzyme-containing **retentate (R)** and low-molecular-weight **filtrate (F)**.
3. Produce a common, defined reconstitution matrix with calibrated buffer, ions, and phosphate.
4. Normalize all treatments for final volume, pH, inorganic-ion composition, phosphate, substrate concentration, retentate protein, and incubation conditions.
5. Test whether R requires F, heat-treated F, or boiled extract for fermentative activity.
6. Test whether pH, salt, phosphate, dilution, protein, or nonspecific stabilization can account for rescue.
7. Repeat with an orthogonal separation method and independently prepared yeast-juice batches.
8. Use initial fermentation rate as the primary quantitative endpoint.

---

# 3. Preparation and quality checks

## 3.1 Independent experimental units

**Proposed units:** independently prepared yeast-juice fermenting preparations, not repeated measurements from one preparation alone.

For each independent preparation:

- Prepare a homogeneous bulk yeast-juice sample.
- Mix gently and immediately split into equal source-volume aliquots before fractionation.
- Maintain all aliquots at a pre-specified cold temperature and processing time window established in a pilot stability study.
- Record source volume, total protein, pH, conductivity, osmolality if available, inorganic phosphate, and a predefined panel of principal inorganic ions.

The exact values are not supplied in the evidence packet and must be **measured and calibrated**, not assumed.

## 3.2 Baseline fermentation qualification

Before fractionation, assay an aliquot of unfractionated yeast juice under a common substrate and atmosphere condition.

A preparation is eligible only if it:

1. Shows a measurable, time-resolved fermentation signal above assay background;
2. Has a reproducible initial rate among technical replicates;
3. Retains activity through the anticipated processing interval.

**Proposed primary fermentation readout:** initial carbon-dioxide production rate, measured continuously or at frequent fixed intervals.

**Proposed secondary readouts:** glucose disappearance and ethanol accumulation, if analytically available. These should corroborate, rather than replace, the gas-production result.

## 3.3 Enzyme-integrity panel

Because loss of retentate activity could reflect enzyme damage rather than loss of a separable factor, monitor:

- Total retentate protein concentration;
- A native protein-profile measure, if available;
- At least two preselected enzyme-marker activities retained in the retentate.

The marker assays should be selected in a calibration phase to be:

1. measurable in unfractionated juice and retentate;
2. sensitive to heat damage or handling injury;
3. demonstrably measurable without adding the candidate filtrate during the marker assay.

If no factor-independent marker can be established, enzyme-damage exclusion will remain incomplete and the conclusion must be weakened accordingly.

---

# 4. Fractionation and independent separation

## 4.1 Primary separation: size-based fractionation

**Proposed method:** ultrafiltration or dialysis-based separation of yeast juice into:

- **R:** high-molecular-weight retentate expected to contain most protein and enzyme activity;
- **F:** low-molecular-weight filtrate/dialysate expected to contain the separable complement.

The molecular-size boundary is an **unreported parameter**. It must be selected in a pilot study using the following operational criteria:

- Most measurable protein and enzyme-marker activity remain in R;
- F contains little or no detectable enzyme-marker activity;
- The separation itself does not abolish R marker activities;
- R and F can be brought to equal final assay conditions without precipitation or excessive dilution.

Record mass balance for volume, protein, phosphate, and principal ions.

## 4.2 Orthogonal separation

To avoid dependence on one membrane, one membrane artifact, or one handling procedure, repeat the experiment using a second, independent separation principle, for example:

- dialysis followed by collection of the external diffusate; or
- size-exclusion chromatography into protein-rich and low-molecular-weight pools.

The second method should use the same defined reconstitution matrix where possible. Its fractions should be tested as an independent experiment, not treated merely as technical replicates of the first method.

**Acceptance criterion for separability:** the key functional contrast must occur with both separation approaches, although exact recovery need not be identical because recovery and dilution may differ.

---

# 5. Defined reconstitution matrix and composition matching

## 5.1 Defined matrix

Prepare a prospectively specified synthetic matrix, **M**, containing:

- A defined noninterfering buffer at calibrated concentration and pH;
- Defined salts supplying each principal inorganic ion measured in the source juice;
- Defined inorganic phosphate, prepared separately from the buffer unless phosphate itself is the chosen buffering species;
- Water and substrate sufficient to bring every treatment to the same final volume and substrate concentration.

The exact buffer identity, concentration, pH, salt species, ion concentrations, phosphate concentration, and substrate concentration are unavailable in the packet. They must therefore be established by calibration:

1. Measure pH, phosphate, and principal ions in unfractionated juice and in R and F after separation.
2. Select a matrix that preserves fermentation of unfractionated juice and does not itself increase or suppress activity.
3. Define acceptable matching tolerances using assay precision and a pilot test of fermentation sensitivity to each variable.
4. Document the full matrix recipe before the confirmatory experiment.

## 5.2 Composition matching at time zero

For every assay mixture, verify at time zero:

- final volume;
- pH;
- total retentate protein amount and concentration;
- phosphate concentration;
- measured principal inorganic ions;
- conductivity or osmolality as a global check;
- substrate concentration.

All mixtures receive the same source-equivalent amount of R. Where F, boiled extract, or analog fractions add volume, every comparison group receives equal compensating matrix volume.

If F contains measurable protein, protein in control mixtures must be matched using either:

1. a factor-depleted, process-matched F fraction with equivalent protein; or
2. a pre-screened inert protein supplement shown not to rescue fermentation by itself.

This addresses the alternative that rescue is caused by nonspecific protein stabilization rather than a separable low-molecular-weight activity.

---

# 6. Treatment allocation and blinding

For each independently prepared yeast-juice batch:

1. Assign aliquots to conditions by random allocation within that batch.
2. Use coded tubes or wells prepared by a person not performing the fermentation readout.
3. Randomize instrument position and reading order.
4. Analyze the primary endpoint using coded condition labels until the analysis plan is finalized.

The analyst should remain blinded at least to the identity of F, heat-treated F, boiled extract, and inactive analog conditions.

---

# 7. Intervention groups

All groups below are proposed and must contain the same final source-equivalent amount of R where R is present, the same final volume, and the same calibrated matrix composition.

## 7.1 Core functional groups

| Group | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Unfractionated yeast juice | Positive preparation-control and benchmark activity. |
| R + M | Tests activity of enzyme-containing fraction after pH, salt, phosphate, and volume correction. |
| F + M | Tests whether filtrate alone contains sufficient enzyme activity or fermentation activity. |
| R + F | Primary reconstitution condition. |
| R + boiled extract (B) | Tests whether a boiled yeast-derived preparation can compensate. |
| R + heat-treated F | Direct heat-stability test of the complement. |
| Heat-treated R + F | Confirms that the enzyme-containing component is heat-labile and that F does not simply supply all enzymatic activity. |

## 7.2 Matrix and inorganic add-back controls

These controls are necessary before interpreting rescue as evidence for a distinct factor.

| Group | Alternative tested |
|---|---|
| R + pH-matched M | pH correction alone. |
| R + ion-matched M lacking added phosphate | Principal inorganic ions without phosphate. |
| R + phosphate add-back in M | Phosphate alone. |
| R + complete ion-plus-phosphate M | Combined salt/phosphate correction. |
| R + F-equivalent water/matrix volume | Dilution or concentration artifact. |
| R + inert protein in M | Nonspecific protein stabilization. |

The “complete ion-plus-phosphate M” condition should reproduce the measured inorganic composition introduced by the F dose, without adding yeast-derived F. If this condition restores activity to the same extent as R + F, a distinct separable factor is not established.

## 7.3 Inactive-analog controls

Two proposed inactive analogs should be included.

1. **Matrix-only inactive analog, A0:** defined M adjusted to match the pH, phosphate, principal ions, conductivity/osmolality, and volume contributed by F, but containing no yeast-derived filtrate.

2. **Process-matched factor-depleted inactive analog, A−:** an F-derived preparation depleted of rescuing activity by an orthogonal depletion or fractionation step identified in pilot work. For example, an orthogonal chromatographic procedure may yield a rescuing pool and a non-rescuing neighboring low-molecular-weight pool. Both must be reconstituted to the same matrix composition.

The second control is stronger but may not be technically achievable without knowing the factor’s chemistry. If it cannot be generated while retaining composition matching, that limitation must be reported rather than hidden.

## 7.4 Heat controls

Heat treatment must be standardized by a temperature log, heating duration, cooling procedure, and evaporation control. Exact conditions should be calibrated prospectively to denature the R enzyme-marker activities while preserving sample volume.

Required checks:

- Measure pH, phosphate, and ion concentrations after heating;
- Restore evaporative losses with defined matrix, not water alone;
- Confirm that heat-treated R loses enzyme-marker activity;
- Compare R + F with R + heat-treated F;
- Compare R + B with R + M and R + complete inorganic M.

A result in which heat-treated F and boiled extract retain rescue, but heat-treated R does not, supports operational heat stability of the complementary activity.

# 8. Sampling and measurements

## 8.1 Fermentation measurements

Measure the primary endpoint over a time course rather than at one endpoint only.

**Primary endpoint:** initial linear fermentation rate, preferably carbon-dioxide evolution rate, expressed both:

- per assay volume; and
- per milligram of retentate protein.

The initial-rate window must be predefined from pilot data, before unblinding.

**Secondary endpoints:**

- lag time to detectable fermentation;
- total carbon dioxide produced over a fixed interval;
- glucose depletion;
- ethanol formation.

Agreement between carbon dioxide, substrate loss, and ethanol production would strengthen interpretation. Disagreement should trigger troubleshooting rather than selective reporting.

## 8.2 Integrity and carryover measurements

At minimum, measure:

- protein in R, F, B, and analogs;
- enzyme-marker activities in unfractionated juice, R, F, heat-treated R, and R after the processing interval;
- pH, phosphate, and principal ions of final mixtures;
- evidence of detectable enzyme-marker carryover into F.

If F contains substantial enzyme-marker activity, apparent rescue could reflect enzyme leakage rather than a nonenzymatic complement; revise the separation before drawing conclusions.

---

# 9. Quantitative primary contrast and analysis

## 9.1 Primary contrast

For each independent yeast-juice preparation, calculate:

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{F}} =
v_{\mathrm{R+F}} - v_{\mathrm{R+A^-}}
\]

where \(v\) is the initial fermentation rate and \(A^{-}\) is the process-matched inactive analog. If A− cannot be validly produced, use the pre-specified fallback contrast:

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{F, fallback}} =
v_{\mathrm{R+F}} - v_{\mathrm{R+A0}}
\]

but label this weaker because A0 does not contain the non-factor organic material present in F.

Supportive planned contrasts are:

\[
v_{\mathrm{R+F}} - v_{\mathrm{R+M}}
\]

\[
v_{\mathrm{R+F}} - v_{\mathrm{R+complete\ inorganic\ M}}
\]

\[
v_{\mathrm{R+heat\text{-}treated\ F}} - v_{\mathrm{R+F}}
\]

and

\[
v_{\mathrm{R+B}} - v_{\mathrm{R+complete\ inorganic\ M}}
\]

## 9.2 Statistical design

Analyze independent preparations as the biological unit, with technical replicates nested within preparation. Use a paired or mixed-effects model with preparation as a blocking factor. Report:

- each preparation’s paired contrasts;
- mean effect and confidence interval;
- recovery relative to unfractionated juice;
- all exclusion failures and failed quality-control runs.

No numerical effect-size threshold is supplied. Before confirmatory work, predefine the minimum biologically meaningful rescue using baseline assay variability, detection limit, and a pilot-derived fraction of unfractionated activity.

# 10. Acceptance criteria and conditional interpretation

## 10.1 Criteria supporting the proposed factor model

The factor model would be supported if all of the following are met:

1. R and F are individually inactive or markedly insufficient, while R + F shows reproducible rescue.
2. F alone has negligible enzyme-marker activity and does not ferment appreciably.
3. R + F exceeds R + A−, or—if A− is unavailable—R + A0, in the prespecified primary contrast.
4. R + F exceeds R plus complete pH-, ion-, phosphate-, and volume-matched matrix.
5. Rescue persists with heat-treated F and/or boiled extract after post-heating composition matching.
6. Heat-treated R loses marker activity and fails to be rescued merely by matrix correction.
7. Retentate protein and enzyme-marker activities are preserved sufficiently to exclude gross handling damage.
8. Inert protein or nonspecific protein matching does not reproduce the rescue.
9. The result recurs using an orthogonal separation method.

## 10.2 Conditional conclusion if criteria are met

> These proposed experiments would support the conclusion that fermentative activity in this yeast-juice system requires, under the assay conditions, an enzyme-containing retained component and a separable heat-stable complementary activity. The evidence would not determine whether the complementary activity is a single compound, a mixture, an organic cofactor, or NAD.

## 10.3 Outcomes that prevent the conclusion

- **Phosphate, salt, or pH add-back restores activity:** the apparent factor may be an inorganic or matrix effect.
- **R + complete inorganic matrix equals R + F:** a distinct yeast-derived complement is not established.
- **F contains enzyme markers or ferments alone:** enzyme leakage or incomplete separation remains plausible.
- **Heat-treated F loses rescue:** the complement is not operationally heat-stable, or heating created an unresolved artifact.
- **Heat-treated R retains normal marker activity but R is inactive:** more specific damage or missing metabolic components remain possible.
- **Inert protein restores activity:** nonspecific stabilization cannot be excluded.
- **Only one separation method works:** membrane, adsorption, or method-specific artifact remains plausible.
- **A− cannot be composition-matched:** the evidence can support complementation but cannot fully exclude all unidentified matrix effects.

---

# 11. Stopping criteria and troubleshooting

## Stop and recalibrate if

- Unfractionated juice loses activity before fractionation is complete;
- R marker activities decline beyond the prospectively calibrated handling tolerance;
- pH, phosphate, ion composition, volume, or protein cannot be matched across conditions;
- F has substantial enzyme carryover;
- heating causes uncontrolled concentration, precipitation, or pH drift;
- technical replicate variation exceeds the pilot-defined assay precision.

## Proposed troubleshooting sequence

1. Shorten processing time and lower handling temperature.
2. Change the molecular-size boundary to improve enzyme retention and reduce F protein carryover.
3. Improve matrix matching using direct phosphate and ion measurements rather than conductivity alone.
4. Use a less disruptive orthogonal separation.
5. Concentrate or dilute fractions only after confirming that concentration procedures do not independently alter fermentation.
6. Re-establish enzyme-marker assays before repeating the functional experiment.

---

## Limits

This protocol can test a functional requirement for a heat-stable, separable activity. It cannot, by itself:

- determine chemical structure;
- establish molecular mass precisely;
- prove that only one factor is involved;
- distinguish all possible low-molecular-weight organic constituents;
- identify the activity as NAD or any other named cofactor.

Those questions require subsequent chemical purification and identity-specific analyses, which are outside the evidence supplied here.
