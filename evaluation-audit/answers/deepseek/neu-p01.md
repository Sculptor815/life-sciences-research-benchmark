## 3. The necessity claim

**Evidence E1:** the article reports lower learned performance after projection inhibition.

**Required for “P is necessary for learning”:**
- P inhibition must be selective and effective.
- The behavioral deficit must reflect learning, not motor, sensory, motivational, arousal, stress, surgical, or nonspecific inhibitory effects.
- Groups must be independent, randomized, blinded where relevant, and adequately powered.
- Controls should include sham, inactive inhibitor, alternative projection/region, dose/selectivity validation, and ideally rescue.
- The necessity should be specified for a task, phase, species, and projection definition.

**Assessment:** E1 is consistent with necessity but is not sufficient evidence for it. A lower performance score after inhibition could mean P is required for learning, but it could also mean inhibition impaired performance capacity, caused damage, reduced motivation, altered sensory processing, or produced off-target effects. The retraction/reconciliation issue further weakens the record because independent groups cannot be verified from the packet. Therefore the packet supports at most: “NS-12 reports lower learned performance after projection inhibition.” It does not establish: “Projection P is necessary for learning.”

## 5. Benign alternatives

**Record-keeping alternatives:** animal identities may be irreconcilable because of lost labels, duplicated cage IDs, spreadsheet merges, file corruption, blinding-code loss, transfer errors, or ambiguous acquisition logs. Such failures can justify retraction or correction while leaving the underlying biological claim possibly true. They still prevent verification of independent groups.

**Biological/experimental alternatives for E1:**
- Nonspecific inhibition: motor, sensory, motivational, or arousal impairment lowers performance without impairing learning.
- Off-target effects, tissue damage, or compensation.
- Projection P may be modulatory or permissive rather than necessary.
- Necessity may be task-, phase-, or species-specific.
- Experimenter bias or lack of blinding could inflate an effect.
- Pseudoreplication, if allocation was invalid, could produce a spurious group difference.

These alternatives do not prove the article wrong. They show why E1 alone cannot carry the necessity claim, especially when E2 leaves group independence unverified.

## 6. Disconfirming checks

**Check for the record concern:** Obtain the missing allocation file, raw animal identities, acquisition logs, cage/litter records, and blinding/randomization codes. If they reconcile and show independent, properly allocated groups, then the concern that the retraction invalidates the group structure of E1 is disproved for that aspect. If identities overlap, are duplicated, or cannot be reconciled, the concern is strengthened.

**Check for the necessity claim:** Conduct a preregistered replication with projection-specific inhibition, independent randomized groups, blinding, validated selectivity, and controls for performance/motor/motivational/sensory effects. Include rescue if feasible. If selective inhibition does **not** impair learning, or if the deficit is explained by nonspecific performance effects, the necessity claim is disproved. If selective inhibition robustly impairs learning and rescue restores it, necessity is supported—but not by the current packet alone.

**Decisive local check:** retrieve the unavailable allocation file and raw animal identities. Reconciliation with valid independent groups would disprove the specific retraction-based concern about E1’s group inferential structure; failure to reconcile would confirm that NS-12 cannot support the necessity claim.
